# Introducing Neon AI Factory Architect

## From Bare Metal to Token

AI infrastructure has become too complex to design with spreadsheets, static diagrams and disconnected engineering documents spread across multiple teams and suppliers.

A modern AI Factory can combine thousands of GPUs, multiple network fabrics, high-performance storage, management systems, user-facing applications and tooling, security controls, power and cooling constraints, deployment dependencies and vendor-specific design rules.

A small mistake in design can become an expensive procurement problem later or the loss of an RFI/RFP bid.

**Neon AI Factory Architect** is designed to catch those problems while they are still design decisions.

## AI Factory architecture, compiled

Neon AI Factory Architect turns infrastructure requirements into a governed, traceable solution architecture.

Instead of creating diagrams, Bills of Material, risk registers and project plans independently, Neon generates them from a common architectural model — one integrated solution architecture and design pack, ready for integration into your RFI response to the AI Factory customer.

That means the architecture can be changed, reassessed and regenerated without manually reconciling every downstream document. Collaboratively, around a single tool that understands AI Factory architecture and deployment.

## From requirements to a bid you can defend

Neon is built for the point where an RFI, RFP, customer requirement or infrastructure concept has to become something technically credible in the form of a coherent solution architecture.

It can bring together:

- GPU and rack-scale architecture
- Ethernet and InfiniBand fabrics
- storage architecture
- management and out-of-band networks
- vendor and technology options
- architecture risk analysis
- Bills of Material and Quantity
- requirements traceability
- project delivery planning
- solution architecture documentation

The goal is not just to generate documents faster. It is to make the engineering itself more repeatable and verifiable.

![Design risk review — expose architecture risks while they are still design decisions](/media/blogs/factory-architect-design-risk.jpg)

## Designed for uncertainty

Real infrastructure projects rarely begin with perfect information.

Some requirements are known. Others are assumptions. Some vendor options are proven. Others require validation.

Neon does not treat missing information as permission to guess.

Where evidence is incomplete, the system can preserve the uncertainty and flag it for human review. Where an alternative requires a different architecture, it remains an explicit branch rather than being presented as a drop-in replacement.

The principle is simple: **Neon presents compliant architecture. You decide.**

Human architects and SMEs retain final authority over the design.

## Your first AI Factory bid should not look like your first

Neon AI Factory Architect is intended to give smaller architecture teams the structure, repeatability and technical discipline normally associated with a much larger specialist practice.

Architecture, GPU platforms, networking, data centre, commercial, compliance, operations and project delivery can all converge through one governed workflow.

## Built for modern accelerated infrastructure

The initial release focuses on current AI Factory technologies and design patterns, including:

- NVIDIA HGX and DGX platforms
- NVLink and NVSwitch (vertical scaling within rack)
- Spectrum-X Ethernet (east-west and storage fabric options)
- InfiniBand (east-west and storage fabric options)
- high-performance AI storage (including vendors like WEKA, VAST, DDN)
- dedicated and converged storage fabrics
- workload and management infrastructure
- security and operational control planes
- project management planning framework derived directly from the architecture

Neon also supports structured vendor-option analysis, distinguishing between selected architecture bases, compatible alternatives, architecture branches and candidates requiring further evidence.
